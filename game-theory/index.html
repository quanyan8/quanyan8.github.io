About the book

The whole elephant

Game theory has become a common language for strategic interaction in computer science, communication networks, energy systems and markets, control, and artificial intelligence. Most textbooks, however, approach it either from economics or from a single application domain, so students from different backgrounds often learn one part of the subject well and miss the rest, like the blind men in the parable who each describe the elephant from the one part they touch.

This book brings the many aspects of game theory together in one framework and one notation: solution concepts and the existence, uniqueness, and refinement of equilibria; computation; incomplete information and mechanism design; bargaining, coalition, and matching games; network, routing, and population games; learning and evolutionary dynamics; and dynamic games in discrete and continuous time.

Dynamic games at the center

Real interactions are rarely one-shot. They unfold over many rounds, in environments that change over time, and what a player knows, and when, can change the nature of an equilibrium. Repeated, Markov, differential, stochastic, Stackelberg, and mean-field games are a core part of the book, not an advanced supplement.

For multi-agent systems and control

Students of multi-agent systems, now often including AI agents that learn through repeated interaction, need this dynamic view. So do students of systems and control, for whom a dynamic game is a control problem with several controllers, each pursuing its own objective. Hence the subtitle.

Shaped in the classroom

The lectures grew out of game theory courses taught for several decades at Illinois and for more than a decade at NYU, to students from engineering, computer science, mathematics, economics, and beyond. Every chapter closes with further notes and reading, and with exercises.
